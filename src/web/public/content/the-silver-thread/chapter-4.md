The house at the river bend was modest and well-kept, with a woodpile stacked against one wall and a pair of shutters that had been recently painted — dark blue, the colour of deep water. Mara stood at the gate for a moment, aware that she had no plan beyond *find me* and a letter from her dead grandmother.

She knocked anyway.

The boy who answered was perhaps a year older than her, dark-haired, wearing an expression of mild surprise that shifted quickly into something more careful. He was holding a spindle. She noticed it the way you notice the obvious detail that confirms a suspicion.

"I'm Mara," she said.

"I know," he said. Then he stopped, as though hearing himself for the first time. "I don't know why I said that. I don't think we've met."

"We haven't," she said.

He opened the door wider.

His name was Soren. He had lived in Threadhaven for fourteen months, come with his father from a town two days north where the weaving trade had dried up like a stream in summer. He was not, he said, particularly good at weaving — he'd been learning late, from books mostly, from watching — but he had a loom because his father had brought it, and he used it because he found it calming, even when the results were uneven.

"Show me," Mara said.

The loom was in the back room, which served as both workroom and sleeping room. It was plain, older than the house it now stood in, worn smooth at the handles from years of other hands. And running through the pale grey wool of his practice cloth — through the warp threads as if it had always belonged there — was a thread the colour of deep water.

Blue. Her blue. The same shade as the warp on her own loom.

Mara crossed to it without asking permission and held her hand near the thread — not touching, just close enough to feel the warmth of it.

It hummed.

"Has it said anything to you?" she asked quietly.

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the river moved against its banks.

"You'll think I'm inventing it," he said.

"I won't."

"It said a name," he said carefully. "A few nights ago, when I was working late. I thought I'd imagined it, and then — it said it again." He looked at her directly. "It said *Mara.*"

She breathed out slowly.

"Mine said *find me,*" she told him. "And before that — nothing. Just a feeling."

He nodded, as though she were confirming something he hadn't dared believe. The morning light through the window was flat and grey and very still.

"My mother used to say," he began, after a while, "that a loom remembers every hand that touches it. That the wool holds the warmth of whoever spun it." He was looking at the blue thread, not at Mara. "I always thought that was just something people say."

"My grandmother left me a letter." Mara took it from her coat and held it out.

He read it standing, without sitting down, the way you read something you half-expected and half-dreaded. When he finished, he folded it carefully along the same worn creases and handed it back.

"Aldric," he said. "That was my grandfather's name."

The silence that followed was not uncomfortable. It was the silence of two people standing at the edge of something large, taking its measure.

Outside, a bird called once and did not repeat itself.

"Do you want tea?" Soren said.

"Yes," said Mara. "I do."

They sat at his small kitchen table and drank the tea while the river went on outside and the blue thread waited in the back room, patient as something that has already waited a long time and knows it can wait a little longer. They talked — about their grandparents, about looms, about the strangeness of arriving at a place you haven't come from and finding that something there already knows your name.

It was, Mara thought, the most honest conversation she had ever had with a stranger.

By the time she walked home, the afternoon light was low and gold against the snow, and the silver thread was exactly where she had left it. But it had relaxed, somehow — the tension in it different, the hum quieter, the way a bell sounds different after it has been answered.
