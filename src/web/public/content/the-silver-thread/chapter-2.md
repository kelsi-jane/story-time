She did not sleep.

That was not unusual — Mara had never been a reliable sleeper — but the quality of this particular wakefulness was different. It wasn't the restless, fidgeting kind, the kind that sent her downstairs for water or outside to count stars. It was still. Alert. Like the feeling before a storm, when the air holds its breath.

She lay in the dark and thought about the thread.

It was still there when she went to check at first light. Of course it was. She had half-convinced herself during the long hours before dawn that she had imagined it — that grief or tiredness or some trick of the winter light had conjured silver where there was only blue. But no. There it was, running through the warp like it had always belonged, like it had been waiting for her to notice it.

She didn't touch it again.

Instead she made tea, sat at the kitchen table, and thought carefully about what she knew.

She knew the thread had not been there the previous evening.

She knew she had heard her name — or something shaped like her name — when she touched it.

She knew that her mother, who had been weaving in this room for thirty years, had never once mentioned silver thread. Not in passing. Not in any of the old stories she told while her hands worked. The stories were full of gold thread and scarlet thread and thread the colour of midnight, but never silver.

*Silver is for binding,* her grandmother had said once, years ago, in the tone of voice that meant she was quoting something older than herself. Mara hadn't asked what that meant. She wished now that she had.

She finished her tea.

Then she went back to the weaving room, sat down at her loom, and — very carefully, very deliberately — reached out and touched the silver thread a second time.

This time, it said two words.

*Find me.*
