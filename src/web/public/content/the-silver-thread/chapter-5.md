*Some things, her grandmother's letter had not said.*

It had not said that the binding was slow — that it happened over weeks, not in a single revelatory moment. It had not said it would feel ordinary, most of the time. That two people connected by an impossible thread could spend a morning arguing about weft tension, or walk back from the market in easy silence, or sit at adjacent looms without speaking because the work asked for quiet and they had both learned to listen to the work.

Mara learned these things on her own.

She and Soren fell into a rhythm the way two instruments fall into tune — not by trying, but by listening. She came to the blue-shuttered house three mornings a week at first, then more, nominally to help him improve his technique but really because the work felt different there. Warmer. More purposeful. As though the thread approved of the proximity.

The silver thread had not spoken again.

But it had moved.

Over those weeks, as Mara wove and Soren practiced beside her, the thread migrated — appearing in her cloth one day, gone the next, present in his warp a week later. It never went far. It seemed to be tracing a continuous line between the two looms, back and forth, as though stitching them together with invisible patience.

"I found something," Soren told her one morning, setting down an old book between them. The cover was water-stained and the spine had been repaired twice with different cord. "An account of a thread called the *bindwarp.* It says it appears when two weavers' intentions align closely enough that the cloth can no longer tell them apart."

"Our intentions," Mara said, without looking up from her work. "What does that mean, exactly?"

"The account is incomplete." He rested his hand on the page. "But I think it means something like — the way you think about a piece while you're making it. What you're trying to put into the cloth. If two people's visions overlap enough, the thread finds the seam between them."

Mara was quiet for a moment. The shuttle moved through the warp, back and forth. "I've been thinking about connection," she said at last. "Since the beginning. Trying to make something that holds."

"I've been thinking about belonging," Soren said, quietly. "Not going anywhere else. Staying."

They looked at each other across the space between the looms.

"That's probably close enough," Mara said.

He smiled — not a large smile, but a real one, the kind that doesn't ask for anything back.

---

Spring arrived without ceremony, the way it always did in Threadhaven: not all at once but in increments, a milder morning here, a green edge at the field's margin there, the river shifting pitch as snowmelt came down from the hills. Mara opened the shutters in the weaving room and for the first time in months the air that entered was soft.

Her mother's loom was working again. Her own was threaded with new wool — not practice cloth, not a plain tabby, but the beginning of something she had designed herself: a long piece in blue and grey, patterned like the sky an hour before dawn, with a fine line of white running through it like light finding a crack.

Or like silver, she thought. Like the memory of silver.

She was finishing the threading when she heard the knock.

Soren stood on the step with two cups of tea — he had begun arriving with tea, a habit that started when she pointed out she always forgot to make it — wearing the expression she had come to know as his thinking face.

"I want to try a joined piece," he said, without preamble. "Two looms, one cloth. I read that it's possible, if the setup is careful enough."

Mara considered this. "It would take most of the day to rig properly."

"I know."

"You'd have to move your loom here."

"I know."

She stepped back from the door.

"Come in, then," she said. "Let's see what the thread thinks."

She might have imagined it. She probably imagined it. But as Soren crossed the threshold, carrying his cup and his thinking face and all the careful intention of a person who has decided where he wants to be, she thought she heard it — both looms, in their separate rooms — hum, just once, in the same key.

She went to put the kettle back on.

Outside, the village was waking. Somewhere down the lane, the mill began its turning. The morning was ordinary and unhurried and full of the particular light that comes when winter has finally finished and the world, for a moment, simply breathes.

The silver thread rested in the cloth, still and bright, doing nothing at all.

It had done enough.
