*Find me.*

The words did not linger. They simply were — present, and then not present, like a candle going out. Mara sat at her loom for a long time afterward, her hand resting near the thread but not quite touching it. The weaving room held its breath around her.

*Find me* was not an instruction she knew how to follow. She had no sense of direction beyond the physical — north road, south field, the river that bent east past the mill. She knew where things were in Threadhaven because she had always known, the way you know the shape of your own hands. But wherever the voice was coming from, it wasn't on any road she'd walked.

She began, for want of any better idea, to weave.

Not with the silver thread — she didn't dare disturb it — but around it, adding new passes of blue wool above and below, working slowly so as not to pull the warp out of true. It was something to do with her hands. Her hands always thought better than her head.

She had been weaving for perhaps an hour when she noticed the pattern.

It wasn't her pattern. She had been making the plain tabby weave her mother had set her, a practice cloth with no design. But the silver thread, as she wove around it, had begun to move — not in any way she could catch directly, but between one glance and the next it shifted, and the shape it made was not abstract.

It was a path.

The thread had traced, in its own language of tension and light, a route through the cloth. A straight line going up through the weave — north, she thought, though the loom had no compass — then a turn. A bend, like a road at a landmark. And at the end of the path, where the thread terminated at the top of the warp, a small shape that might have been a house, or a loom, or both.

Mara pressed her thumb gently to the cloth and felt it hum.

She thought of what her grandmother had said, years ago, in the tone of voice reserved for old things: *Silver is for binding.* And then, for the first time, she thought to ask what that meant. What, exactly, it was for binding.

She went to the chest under the stairs — her grandmother's chest, which she had been told as a child contained only old linens — and opened it.

Beneath the linens, there was a letter.

The paper was old, the ink faded to the colour of rain on stone, but the handwriting was clear. Her grandmother's hand. The same careful, slightly tilted script that Mara had spent years learning to imitate.

*If you have found this,* the letter began, *then the thread has found you. Don't be frightened. It found me too, once. And everything that came from it was good.*

*His name, when I knew him, was Aldric. He lived at the north end of the village, near the bend in the river. He had a loom the colour of winter sky and he didn't understand the thread any better than I did, and that not-knowing, shared between us, turned out to be enough.*

*Look for the loom. Look for the one who doesn't understand.*

*The thread knows the rest.*

Mara read it twice, slowly, then a third time quickly. Then she folded it along its existing creases, tucked it into her coat, and stepped out into the cold.

The north end of the village was twenty minutes' walk. She had been there before — she had been everywhere in Threadhaven, it was not a large place — but she had never paid particular attention to the houses near the river bend. There was the mill. A few older cottages. And, she now recalled, a newer building that had gone up in the last two years, belonging to a family who had come from somewhere else.

Her breath made small clouds in the winter air.

She walked north.
