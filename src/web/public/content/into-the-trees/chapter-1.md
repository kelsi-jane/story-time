# The Pull of the Trees

Okay, so here's the thing about being stuck inside on a day that is *objectively perfect* — and I mean perfect, like, crisp-air, orange-leaves, the-whole-world-smells-like-cinnamon perfect — it's basically a crime against nature.

I'm serious. I stood at my bedroom window for probably twenty minutes just staring at the woods behind the house, watching the leaves come loose in little spirals and thinking, *why am I in here?* The answer, obviously, was: school stuff, chores, the general invisible force that keeps teenagers indoors against their will. But honestly? None of that felt like a good enough reason on a day like this.

The woods have always been back there. My whole life. I could see them from the kitchen, from the backyard, from my bedroom if I pressed my nose against the glass — which I definitely did more than once. They're not some big mysterious wilderness. It's probably thirty or forty acres of mixed oaks and pines with a creek somewhere in the middle, the kind of patch of woods that just exists between subdivisions, forgotten by everyone except the squirrels. But every fall, when the leaves turn and the light goes golden and the whole thing looks like something out of a painting, I'd think: *I should go in there.*

I never did, though. Not alone. I'd poke around the edges with my neighbor's dog sometimes, but I always turned back before the trees got thick.

Today was different.

I don't know exactly what flipped the switch. I'd been feeling that restless thing — you know the one, where your skin feels two sizes too small and the walls of your house feel like they're slowly closing in, and everything you'd normally do just feels flat and hollow. I'd tried music. I'd tried rearranging my room. I'd tried baking, which mostly resulted in a smoke alarm situation.

So I put on my boots.

I told myself I was just going to the edge. Just to stand there and breathe. Five minutes, maybe. And then I'd go back in.

Ha.

The second I stepped off the lawn and into the first real shade of the trees, something happened that I can only describe as: my whole body went *oh, yes, this.* Like I'd been holding my breath for months without knowing it and I finally just... exhaled. The light came through the canopy in slanted gold bars, and the leaves were every shade of red and amber and this deep, burnished orange that I don't think has a name yet. It smelled like earth and cold air and something faintly sweet, like the whole forest was in the middle of a slow, comfortable exhale of its own.

I went further.

Of course I did.

I followed a deer trail I wasn't sure was actually a deer trail, stepping over roots and ducking under low branches, leaves crunching underfoot in that incredibly satisfying way that makes you want to keep walking just to keep hearing it. At some point I crossed into the part of the woods where you can't see the house anymore, and instead of being nervous, I just felt... free. Genuinely, ridiculously free — the way that only happens when nobody can see you and nobody is asking you anything and you don't have to be any particular version of yourself.

I climbed a mossy rock. I found a feather — long and cream-colored, speckled at the tip. I held it for a while and thought about how something had to lose it and something else, maybe me, was supposed to find it. I know, I know. That's just how my brain works, okay?

And then I found the footprints.

Small, neat, four-toed — pressed clean into a soft patch of mud near the base of a big oak. Too big for a cat. Too small for a coyote. *Fox,* I thought, the word surfacing from some half-remembered nature documentary. The prints were heading deeper in, following the natural curve of the land downhill.

I looked back in the direction of the house. Then I looked at the prints.

Then I followed them.
