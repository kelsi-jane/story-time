# The Log Bridge

"I am absolutely not doing this."

I said this, and then I did it anyway. Which is, now that I think about it, sort of the story of my life.

The log was wider than I'd first thought — maybe eight inches across at its widest point — and mostly submerged, with about three inches sticking up above the current. It was mossy and dark and absolutely, definitely, one hundred percent slippery. The water pushed against the downstream side in little arcs of white. It was not a bridge. It was a very long coincidence that happened to be made of wood.

I stood at the edge of the bank for a solid minute, toes right at the waterline, staring at it.

"You could still go around," Knox said helpfully, from behind me.

"You've mentioned."

"Just keeping the option alive."

"Knox," Autumn said.

"What?"

"*Shh.*"

I took off one boot. I don't know why — some instinct that bare foot might grip better than rubber sole. I looked at my boot in my hand. I put it back on. That was clearly insane. I looked at Pip on the other bank, who was grooming his face with his tiny paws and showing absolutely no concern for my situation.

"How did you even do that?" I called over.

"Short legs," he said, like this was a complete explanation.

I thought about every time I'd stood at the edge of something and talked myself back from it. The high dive at the public pool when I was twelve. The audition I'd almost bailed on. The first day of every school year, pausing on the sidewalk and finding some reason to delay. I had a real talent for *almost.*

Maybe I was tired of almost.

I stepped onto the log.

Cold water rushed immediately over my boot. The current was fast — I could feel it pushing against my foot, trying to nudge me sideways — and the log shifted slightly under my weight with a grinding sound that launched my heart directly into my throat. I threw my arms out wide like a tightrope walker. Behind me, Knox made a sound that might have been concern.

"Don't watch if it stresses you out!" I called.

"It doesn't stress me out," he said. "I'm curious."

Somehow, that helped. I took another step. The log held. Another, arms still out, one foot at a time, full ridiculous tightrope posture, and the water was cold and the log was slippery and I was absolutely going to make it and I was also maybe definitely possibly not going to make it —

I stumbled onto the far bank.

I grabbed a branch, caught my balance, and stood there with my heart hammering and my boots soaked and mud on both hands, and I *laughed.* Not a polished laugh — a real one, the kind that just arrives without asking. Because I'd done it, and it was so absurd, and the water was just water and the log was just a log and I was on the other side.

Autumn took it at a full run. Obviously. She cleared the whole thing in four bounds and shook herself off next to me like this was nothing, already looking ahead toward the trees. Knox crossed last, with absolutely infuriating grace, like the log had been placed there specifically for him.

"There you go," he said.

"Was that the lesson? Was that your whole plan all along?"

He tilted his head — that particular tilt that meant he wasn't going to answer directly. "I've been watching you find the way all afternoon," he said. "Every every time you thought you were lost, you found it again." He looked at me, no joke in it. "You've been doing this the whole time. You just needed to see it."

I stood there with wet boots and muddy hands in the gold light on the far bank, and something settled in me. Not a big dramatic thing. A quiet thing, like a door clicking shut in exactly the right way. Like a room I hadn't known was cluttered had gotten a little tidier.

I looked at the trees ahead — thicker, older, the light going amber between the trunks.

"Okay," I said. "Where's the tree?"

"Close," Knox said. And for once, he didn't add anything else.
