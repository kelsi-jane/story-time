# Knox, Luna, and a Very Bad Plan

The fox prints led me to a little hollow between two oaks where the ground dipped and the roots made a kind of natural seat, and that is where I met Knox.

He was sitting on a root like he owned it, one ear cocked sideways, watching me with amber eyes that were entirely too amused for a fox. He was the deep rust-red color of a good autumn, with a white chest and the kind of expression that said *I've been waiting for you, and also I've already thought of three jokes.*

"You've been staring at these woods for years," he said. "Took you long enough."

I stopped walking. I processed this. A fox was talking to me.

"Okay," I said.

"That's it? Okay?"

"I mean — yes? You're talking. Okay."

He tilted his head. "Most people run."

"I read a lot of books," I said. "Talking animals aren't that surprising when you read a lot of books."

Knox made a sound that was absolutely a laugh. He stood up, stretched the way animals do — that full-body, front-paws-forward stretch — and then looked at me with what I can only describe as genuine delight. "I think I'm going to like you," he said. "My name's Knox. And before you ask — no, I don't know where your socks go."

"That was terrible."

"I have more."

"I believe you."

He trotted over to a gap between the trees and made a sharp bark-chirp that I supposed was a whistle, and that's when Autumn came crashing out of the underbrush like a small furry meteor.

She was smaller than Knox, with patches of amber and white and eyes the color of pine sap, and she moved the way very caffeinated people move — all energy, no brakes. She looked me over once, decided something in about half a second, and said, "Oh, she's perfect, Knox, she's going to *love* it —"

"Love what?" I said.

"The Heart Tree." She said it like it was obvious, like she'd already told me three times.

I looked at Knox. "The Heart Tree?"

He sat down and did this philosophical ear-flick. "An old oak. Dead center of the woods, if the stories are right. Supposedly, if you find it and sit in its roots long enough, the woods whisper something back to you." He paused. "Or so they say."

"Do you believe it?"

"I believe it makes for an excellent walk." He grinned — I didn't know foxes could grin, but this one absolutely could. "Also, I need to check on something in that direction anyway."

"Acorns," Autumn said flatly.

"I call them *provisions.*"

"And I'll be there," said a new voice, quiet as a sigh, from somewhere above me.

I looked up. On a branch about twelve feet overhead, half-hidden in the shade of a golden canopy, sat an owl — snow-white, enormous, watching me with eyes the color of a winter moon. She had the kind of stillness that made everything around her seem a little calmer, like she was the center of gravity in the whole forest.

"Luna," Knox said, by way of introduction.

"Hi," I said, because what else do you say to an owl.

"You found us," Luna said simply. Her voice was low and warm, like a fireplace in a small room. "That's the first step."

Autumn was already spinning in an impatient circle. "Can we *go* now? The light is perfect and the leaves are perfect and —"

"Yes," I said. "Let's go."

I surprised myself a little with that. But standing there in a hollow between a philosophical fox, a whirlwind fox, and a silent white owl in a forest full of October light, I wasn't nervous at all. I was exactly the opposite of nervous. I was the feeling you get right before something good.

We went.
