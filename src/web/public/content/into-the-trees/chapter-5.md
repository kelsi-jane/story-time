# The River That Wasn't

I want to be clear about something: in my head, it was basically the Amazon River.

In reality — and I can admit this now — it was probably a six-or-seven-foot-wide creek that had gotten a serious attitude from recent rain. But in the moment, standing at the bank with the cold smell of moving water hitting my face and the sound of it running fast and brown over rocks, my brain made a very different assessment.

The water was high. I could see the normal waterline on the stones — pale and dry a foot above where the current was rushing now, which meant the creek had swollen pretty significantly after whatever storms had rolled through earlier in the week. It moved fast, too. You could see little chops and eddies where it hit the bigger rocks, and in a few places it frothed white. There was a low, constant sound to it that my brain helpfully translated as: *danger, danger, danger.*

My imagination, which is usually an asset, was absolutely not helping.

I pictured stepping in and being swept immediately away. I pictured hypothermia. I pictured — and I'm not proud of this — a crocodile, which logically I knew was absurd in the deciduous woods of the mid-Atlantic, but my brain had gone fully off-leash by this point and was just putting things out there.

"It's not that bad," Autumn said, and then immediately started looking for a spot to jump across, which was not comforting at all.

Knox sat at the bank and regarded the creek like it was a philosophical problem he was genuinely enjoying. He said nothing. Also not comforting.

I looked up and down the bank for a bridge that wasn't there. I looked for stepping stones — all submerged. I looked for a narrow spot — the channel seemed to widen in both directions, which felt personal.

"What if we go around?" I said.

"Could do," Knox said. "It loops, though. Long way."

"How long?"

"Forty minutes, maybe fifty." He paused. "Also, I noticed some very promising-looking oak trees in that direction."

"That doesn't help me, Knox."

"It helps me."

I crossed my arms. The clouds were doing that late-afternoon thing where they turned everything soft and a little melancholy, and the creek was loud and the forest suddenly felt very large, and I felt — just for a moment — very small inside it. Like I'd gotten too far out and the comfortable distance between me and home had stretched past some invisible line.

Maybe this was the part where I turned back.

"What does the water actually need from you?"

Luna. I hadn't even seen her land — she was just there on a branch above the bank, white as birch bark, watching me with those moon-pale eyes.

I looked at the water. I turned her question over. It made no immediate logical sense.

The water didn't need anything from me. It was just... moving. It didn't care that I was here. It wasn't trying to block me or threaten me or make a point — it was just doing what water does, which is go downhill at whatever speed the weather has recently made possible. It wasn't my enemy. It was just a creek.

"It doesn't need anything," I said slowly. "It's just water."

Luna blinked once, slowly, like a period at the end of a sentence.

And that's when Pip shot past my ankles in a brown-and-white blur, barely pausing at the bank before launching himself onto something just below the waterline — and then bounding across to the other side, where he landed, sat down, and puffed his cheeks at us like he'd done something extraordinary.

I stared at where he'd run. Half-hidden by the brown water, mostly submerged, almost completely invisible unless you knew to look: a log, spanning most of the width. Solid and old and absolutely, unmistakably there.

"Oh," I said.

Autumn appeared at my shoulder. "Oh," she agreed.

We both looked at Knox.

"I was going to mention it," he said.
