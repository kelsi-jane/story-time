# The Woods That Spin

Here's the thing nobody tells you about following a fox: they don't walk in straight lines.

Knox meandered. He looped around interesting stumps and doubled back on himself to sniff things and went slightly uphill for a while and then slightly downhill, and the whole time Autumn was skipping ahead and falling back and disappearing around bends and I was somewhere in the middle trying to keep track of where the sun was, which is harder than it sounds when you're under a tree canopy and everything is the same golden-brown.

We'd been walking maybe forty-five minutes when I noticed the oak.

Crooked trunk, split about six feet up into two branches — one pointing left, one pointing right, like it was permanently shrugging. I'd noticed it earlier because of the split. I noticed it again because of the split. Because it was the same tree.

"Knox," I said.

"Mmm."

"Have we passed that tree before?"

He glanced at it. "Which one?"

"The shrug tree. The one that looks like it's shrugging."

He considered this for a moment. "...Define 'passed.'"

"Oh my gosh. Are we going in circles?"

"Spirals," he corrected. "More of a spiral. It's a very efficient pattern for —"

"Finding acorns?" Autumn said, not even looking up.

"For *surveying terrain,*" Knox said, with great dignity.

I stopped walking. I put my hands on my hips, which is what I do when I'm trying to get my brain organized, and looked around. The woods looked the same in every direction — beautiful and amber and completely, totally unhelpful. The sun had shifted. I wasn't sure which way we'd come from anymore. The trees crowded closer, or maybe I was just noticing them more, and the wind picked up out of nowhere and sent a whole shower of leaves spiraling down around us like the forest was trying to make a point.

And then the light changed.

One second it was golden and warm; the next, clouds slid over the sun and everything went flat gray, and the shadows between the trees went from friendly to *hmm* real fast. Branches creaked. The wind moved through the high canopy and made this low, whispering sound that my brain immediately started interpreting as: *something is watching you.*

I am a perfectly rational person.

I was also, suddenly, a little bit terrified.

"Did you hear —"

*CRACK.*

Something in the underbrush to my left moved fast. I actually yelped — a real, embarrassing yelp — and jumped sideways, and Knox spun toward the sound with lightning speed, and for one very dramatic second we were all frozen.

Then a chipmunk fell out of a low branch and landed in the leaves directly in front of me.

He sat up, looked around, patted his cheeks like he was checking his pockets, and then stared up at me with enormous black eyes.

"Sorry," he said cheerfully. "Missed the branch. I do that."

Autumn burst out laughing. Knox made a sound that was definitely a sigh disguised as a cough. I pressed my hand to my chest like my heart had physically relocated there.

"I'm Pip," the chipmunk said. "You look scared. Were you scared? You looked scared."

"I wasn't —"

"She was scared," Pip confirmed to Knox.

"A little," Knox agreed.

I sat down on a log. Not on purpose — my knees just kind of decided to do that. Luna drifted down from somewhere above and landed on a branch a few feet away, looking at me the way she always seemed to — steady and quiet and like she knew something she wasn't rushing to share.

"You okay?" she asked.

"Yeah," I said. And then: "Are we actually lost?"

Luna didn't answer right away. She tilted her head, slow and patient, and said: "What do *you* think?"

I looked at the shrug tree. I looked at the sky, gray and close. I looked at Pip, who had already moved on and was investigating a pine cone with great seriousness.

Honestly? I thought we might be. But I also thought that lost wasn't necessarily the end of anything — it was just a different kind of start.
